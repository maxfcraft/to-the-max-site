Marketing for HVAC companies

HVAC advertising is not really about the tune-up. It is about owning the relationship with a homeowner whose system is eight years old, so that when it dies in July you are the number they already have.

01 / The problem

What goes wrong when HVAC companies advertise

The emergency call is the worst customer you will ever have. They are panicked, they are price shopping three companies at once, and they will never call again. Every HVAC company built on emergency calls is starting from zero every summer.

The tune-up offer is not a profit centre and was never supposed to be. It is a way to get a technician in front of an aging system. Companies that treat it as a margin product price it too high, advertise it badly, and get nothing.

Replacement is where the money is and almost nobody advertises to it properly, because the trigger is invisible. You cannot see a fifteen-year-old furnace from the road. You can only reach it by being in front of that homeowner for months before it fails.

02 / The build

What To The Max builds for HVAC companies

  1. A seasonal tune-up offer, priced to get a tech through the door, running ahead of the season rather than during it. Cooling in April, heating in September.

  2. A maintenance plan pitched at the visit, not in the ad, because the plan is what turns one call into a decade of revenue.

  3. A separate replacement and financing campaign aimed at older homes, with monthly payment framing instead of a five-figure sticker.

  4. Retargeting everyone who has ever hit the site or watched the video, because the actual trigger is a failure you cannot schedule. When it happens you want to be the name already in their head.

  5. Emergency campaigns that switch on when the weather does, because bidding for emergency intent in mild weather is spending money on nothing.

04 / Questions

Questions HVAC companies ask

Do Facebook ads work for HVAC companies?

Yes, for tune-ups, maintenance plans, and replacement, which is most of the profit. They work poorly for true emergencies, where the homeowner searches Google with a broken system and buys in ten minutes. Run Meta to build the pipeline and to own the customer before the failure, and let search catch the panic calls.

How much does an HVAC lead cost?

Twenty to sixty dollars for a tune-up lead, and one hundred to three hundred for a replacement lead. The tune-up looks better on a report and the replacement pays the bills. Judge them separately, because averaging them together hides which campaign is actually funding the business.

Is a cheap tune-up offer worth running?

Yes, as long as you understand what you are buying. The tune-up is not a profit product. It is paid access to an aging system and a homeowner who now knows your technician by name. Companies that price it to make money on the visit get almost no takers and lose the replacement revenue behind it.

When should HVAC ads run during the year?

Ahead of the season, not inside it. Cooling offers in April, heating offers in September. Advertising a tune-up in the first heat wave means competing with every emergency dollar in the market at the worst possible cost. The companies with full summer calendars booked them in spring.

How do you sell system replacements with ads?

Separately, with monthly payment framing and a financing option stated up front. A five-figure number in an ad stops the scroll for the wrong reason. A monthly figure with a free load calculation or in-home estimate gets the appointment, and the appointment is where a replacement is actually sold.
